# Evaluating XeSS against the shipped FSR2

The question: should the mod adopt Intel XeSS, either as anti-aliasing in place of FSR2 or as a VR upscaler that renders each eye below the headset's native resolution? The premise behind asking is that XeSS is newer than FSR 2.2.1, so its image quality may be better.

**Recommendation: no, but not because it cannot be done.** It can, and the premise is not baseless: XeSS probably does reconstruct better than FSR 2.2.1 on average, and its API solves a stereo problem FSR2's structurally cannot. It loses on cost and on effort: reaching it from a D3D11 game on non-Intel hardware requires a D3D11on12 bridge with a reported 10–15% frametime penalty, XeSS is the most expensive of the three upscalers on its DP4a path, and the frame is not fill-bound at the resolutions measured (`performance.md`), so a more expensive upscaler would be paid for with the resource that is actually scarce. Meanwhile the shipped FSR2 integration's remaining work is not upscaler-specific: all of it would have to be done again, unchanged, for XeSS.

Do this instead, in order: finish the FSR2 upscaler that issue #13 already scoped, validate the foveation that is already shipped and on by default (issue #29), and only then revisit the upscaler *version* — where the right candidate is FSR 3.1, not XeSS. The relevant prior art is issue #13 (FSR upscaling), issue #29 (foveation and the alternatives survey), issue #10 (the per-eye flicker that drove the motion-vector work), and issue #34 (the profiler that produced `performance.md`).

Reopen this if the mod ever targets Intel Arc as a first-class platform, or if the eye seam under the single-pass collapse becomes the binding quality problem — that is the one thing XeSS can do and FSR2 cannot.

## The D3D11 gate is real, and it is a driver passthrough

Verified from the XeSS SDK at `github.com/intel/xess`.

There is a `xess_d3d11.h`, and it carries the note *"XeSS D3D11 version only works on Intel Hardware."* Intel's XeSS-SR developer guide is blunter: D3D11 support is *"limited to Intel® Arc™ Graphics or later. Only an Intel-optimized implementation is available. Creating a context on non-supported devices, e.g., non-Intel devices or Intel® Iris® Xe, will fail with `XESS_RESULT_ERROR_UNSUPPORTED_DEVICE`."*

The mechanism explains why this is not a policy that might soften. `libxess_dx11.dll` is 156 KB and imports neither `d3d11.dll` nor `d3d12.dll`; it loads `igdext64.dll` (Intel Graphics Extensions) and calls `INTC_D3D11_XessCreateContext` / `INTC_D3D11_XessExecute`. The whole implementation lives inside the Intel driver, and the DLL is a shim to it. The cross-vendor `libxess.dll` is 77 MB, delay-imports `d3d12.dll`, and contains a "Generic DXIL>SPIR-V translation error" string — it carries DXIL kernels requiring Shader Model 6.4 (`XESS_RESULT_ERROR_UNSUPPORTED_DEVICE`: "An SM 6.4 capable GPU is required"). D3D11 consumes DXBC at SM 5.x, so those kernels cannot be created on a D3D11 device at all.

Nor can we recompile them down, the way the community FSR2 DX11 port recompiled FSR's open HLSL into the 124 SM5 permutations in `fsr-sys/generated/dx11`. The SDK is binary-only under the Intel Simplified Software License: redistribution in binary form is permitted (better than expected), but *"No reverse engineering, decompilation, or disassembly of the Software is permitted, nor any modification or alteration of the Software or its operation at any time, including during execution."*

A corollary worth recording: the D3D11 backend would not work here **even on an Arc GPU**, because `igdext64.dll` is a Windows Intel driver component that does not exist in a Wine prefix. The native D3D11 path is Windows-and-Intel, not merely Intel.

This confirms the claim already recorded in [`fsr.md`](fsr.md) against primary sources. What it does *not* do is end the evaluation.

## The D3D12 path is reachable, contrary to my first assessment

An earlier revision of this document assessed cross-API interop as impractical and stated that no report of anyone doing it existed. **That was wrong**, and the correction matters more than the original claim did.

**OptiScaler does exactly this, in production, for this class of mod.** Its documented backends are `XeSS 2.X (native DX11, Intel ARC only)` versus `XeSS, FSR 2.1.2, 2.2.1, FSR 3.X w/Dx12 (via D3D11on12)`, footnoted: *"These implementations use a background DirectX12 device to be able to use DX12-only upscalers. There's a performance penalty up to 10-ish % for this method."* Their wiki puts the range at 10–15%. The shape is a known, shipped one: stand up a background D3D12 device, share the engine's textures across the boundary, run XeSS there.

**Proton is not the blocker either.** DXVK carries a dedicated XeSS workaround in `src/dxgi/dxgi_options.cpp` — it hides the Intel vendor ID from applications that have loaded `libxess`/`libxess_dx11`, gated on the DLL's product version being below 2.0.2.68 — which presupposes the DP4a path working on non-Intel under Proton. DXVK PR #5798, authored by an Intel engineer, narrowed that spoofing for XeSS 2 and mentions a planned Proton shim; DXVK 3.0 drops the spoof for XeSS 2 entirely. GE-Proton ships `PROTON_XESS_UPGRADE` and `libxess_dx11.dll` upgrade support; Proton-CachyOS integrated OptiScaler behind `PROTON_USE_OPTISCALER=1`. XeSS on non-Intel hardware under Proton is a well-trodden path, and this repo's prefix already provisions the native `d3dcompiler_47.dll` that covers the one documented Linux shader-compilation gotcha OptiScaler reports.

So the honest position is **viable, at a price**. Two caveats on that price, in both directions. Against XeSS: it stacks on top of XeSS's own cost, on its slow path, for a frame that is not short of fill rate. In its favour: OptiScaler's 10–15% comes from GPU-bound flatscreen titles, and this frame is draw-submission-bound with the GPU near 50% (`performance.md`), so a background D3D12 device's overhead may land quite differently here — possibly better, possibly worse, since the cost is partly CPU-side submission and that *is* our scarce resource. It is measurable early and cheaply, and it is the first thing to measure if this is ever reopened.

## XeSS's real advantage, stated fairly

XeSS has one capability FSR2 lacks outright, and it lands precisely on the mod's hardest VR problem.

Single-pass stereo renders both eyes into one double-wide target. A temporal pass run once over that target samples neighbourhoods across the eye seam, where adjacent texels belong to different eyes with unrelated history — already recorded as a known gap for FSR in `single-pass-stereo.md`. The collapse's per-eye tool, `reconstruction::split_fullscreen_pass`, masks with a **scissor**, and a scissor clips rasterisation while doing nothing to a dispatch. Both FSR2 and XeSS are entirely compute, so the machinery the mod already has cannot split either.

`xess_d3d12_execute_params_t` and `xess_d3d11_execute_params_t` both carry `inputColorBase`, `inputMotionVectorBase`, `inputDepthBase`, `inputResponsiveMaskBase`, and `outputColorBase` — `xess_coord_t` sub-rectangle offsets, present unchanged since v1.0.0. Two contexts with different base offsets upscale the two halves of a double-wide target independently, with no copies. `FfxFsr2DispatchDescription` has `renderSize` and no offsets at all; inputs must start at (0,0). Per-eye FSR2 under the collapse therefore means `CopySubresourceRegion`-ing each eye's colour, depth, and velocity into scratch and copying the result back — three half-target reads and one half-target write per eye, about four full-frame copies per frame of pure overhead.

**There is a shipping VR precedent for exactly this.** PureDark's Skyrim Upscaler (VR branch) is a D3D11 game with a double-wide side-by-side target; it runs two upscaler instances against the same source textures with `colorBase`/`depthBase`/`motionBase` set to the left and right halves, then composites the two per-eye outputs back. That is the design this mod would need, already demonstrated.

Two contexts are mandatory either way — XeSS *"maintains an internal history state to perform temporal accumulation"*, one history per context, and the SDK is single-thread-affine (all calls from the initialising thread, which is fine here: the dispatch already lives on the render thread). Intel's Unreal plugin confirms XeSS-SR supports VR while XeSS-FG explicitly does not, *"due to XeSS SDK API limitations"*. Frame generation is a non-starter here regardless: the OpenXR compositor owns reprojection and pacing, and a generated frame carries no correct head pose.

## Testing the premise: is "newer" actually better?

Probably, but weakly, and less than the framing suggests.

The general consensus across independent per-game comparisons is that XeSS sits **between DLSS and FSR** on quality — softer than DLSS overall, more temporally stable than FSR2. But it does not hold uniformly, and the exceptions are the kind VR would notice most. TechPowerUp's per-title series found XeSS 1.3 DP4a *free of ghosting, shimmering, or disocclusion artifacts* in one game, showing **more vegetation shimmering than the alternatives, still visible at 4K Quality** in another, and carrying **the most ghosting and smearing on small flying objects, not improved by higher resolution** in a third. Quality is strongly implementation-dependent; there is no single characteristic failure mode, and "XeSS is more stable" is a tendency rather than a property. Vegetation shimmer and small-mover smearing are precisely this game's content.

The version picture also complicates the comparison rather than settling it. XeSS 1.3 was a substantive model change — *"reduced ghosting, improved temporal stability"* is Intel's own wording and therefore **marketing** — and it silently remapped presets to scale factors (Quality 1.5× → 1.7×), so same-preset comparisons across versions are not like for like. `xessForceLegacyScaleFactors` exists to undo that.

So: plausibly better than FSR 2.2.1 in the average case, on evidence that is game-dependent, flatscreen, and not measured on the axis that matters here. That is a reason to be curious, not a reason to migrate.

The cost side is where it turns. XeSS is the heaviest of the three. TechSpot/Hardware Unboxed, testing XeSS 1.0 on an RTX 3060, found *"the advantage of rendering at a lower resolution is eaten up entirely by the overhead of the XeSS upscaler"* at Ultra Quality; performance-normalised, XeSS Balanced ≈ DLSS Quality ≈ FSR2 Quality — roughly **one full quality tier** of cost on Ampere. TechPowerUp's aggregate puts XeSS 10–13% behind DLSS/FSR on the compatibility kernel, and OptiScaler's own wiki states *"XeSS is heavier than FSR for GPUs, so it's expected to have lower performance even on Intel Arc GPUs."* Those figures predate XeSS 1.3's rebalancing and nobody re-ran the methodology afterwards, so treat them as directional rather than current.

**No ms-per-pass figures for XeSS DP4a on NVIDIA hardware exist at any resolution.** Two independent searches found none with any methodology behind them; what search returns is content-farm output and should not be quoted. That number would have to be measured here, twice per frame, before any performance argument could be settled. Stack the unknown against the 10–15% interop tax, and against the fact that on this GPU XeSS runs its DP4a network — a genuinely different, lighter-trained model than the XMX one, not the same network running slower — and the quality advantage is being bought with the resource the profiler says is not the constraint.

The other half of the premise: **"newer" is available inside the FSR line without any of this.** FSR 3.1's upscaler is still plain SM6.2 compute with no ML hardware gate, still MIT, with an explicit native-AA mode and the cleaner `ffxapi` interface, and it has a community DX11 backend. It was the mod's original target and lost only on port maturity under Proton (`fsr.md`). Its headline improvements over 2.2 are temporal stability and ghosting — the same axis XeSS wins on — and it needs no interop layer, no proprietary DLL, and no re-plumbing. FSR 4 stays disqualified (RDNA4-gated ML, never runs on NVIDIA or Intel).

## What the switch would actually cost

None of the following is paid down by choosing XeSS over FSR2. It is the same work either way, and most of it is already done for FSR2.

**The input set is the same, so nothing transfers away.** XeSS takes colour, velocity (`R16G16_FLOAT`), depth (required unless `XESS_INIT_FLAG_HIGH_RES_MV`), an optional 1×1 exposure scale, an optional responsive-pixel mask, jitter in `[-0.5, 0.5]`, and a history-reset flag — FSR2's set. Everything hard-won in `fsr/velocity_decode.rs` (decoding JC3's bias-encoded 8-bit velocity buffer, the per-eye previous-view-projection re-anchoring, the jitter cancellation) would be rebuilt against XeSS's conventions, which are mostly configuration rather than insight: motion in screen-space **pixels**, unjittered by default with `XESS_INIT_FLAG_JITTERED_MV` available to let XeSS remove jitter itself, `XESS_INIT_FLAG_USE_NDC_VELOCITY`, and `XESS_INIT_FLAG_INVERTED_DEPTH` for our reverse-Z.

Two small genuine wins hide in there. `JITTERED_MV` is nicer than FSR2's requirement that we hand over clean vectors — worth a few dozen lines, not a migration. And XeSS wants no near/far/FOV/frame-time, which removes the per-eye asymmetric-frustum vertical-FOV approximation `fsr.md` flags as an open VR caveat.

Against them, four requirements the current integration does not meet:

- **XeSS wants HDR pre-tonemap colour.** It tonemaps internally and is tuned for that; `XESS_INIT_FLAG_LDR_INPUT_COLOR` exists with an explicit quality warning attached. The mod dispatches FSR **post**-tonemap at the engine's AA hook, which is the SMAA drop-in (`fsr.md`, "Where it dispatches"). Adopting XeSS effectively forces the pre-tonemap path — `fsr.md`'s order-of-work step 6, deferred and not built — so the dispatch-point work would have to be done first, and it is the wiring-heavy option.
- **A mip bias is required**, `-1` at 2.0×, and there is no mip-bias plumbing in the mod today. That is engine-side sampler work on a 2015 deferred renderer, not an upscaler-side setting.
- **XeSS supplies no jitter sequence** (FSR2 gives us `ffxFsr2GetJitterOffset` and `ffxFsr2GetJitterPhaseCount`, documented Halton(2,3)), and the sequence length must scale with the **square** of the scale factor — ≥72 phases at 3.0×. Note the mod currently ships with jitter **off** by default because it excites a scene-side shadow flicker that survived a long bisection (`fsr.md`, "Jitter status"). XeSS without jitter would be a worse trade than FSR2 without it, since more of XeSS's quality claim rests on sub-pixel reconstruction.
- **History resets cost convergence**, and VR generates them: recentering, fast travel, and the loading-screen camera parking (issue #27) are all view discontinuities. XeSS's longer history makes each reset more visible, not less.

**Wrong motion vectors remain the dominant failure mode.** `single-pass-stereo.md` records that bark's velocity pass reprojects only the current-frame matrix while the previous-frame copy at `cb1[5..8]` stays centred, so bark's vectors are wrong under the collapse. At native AA that is shimmer on tree trunks; at reduced render scale it is smearing that tracks the camera, on foliage, in a jungle game, at 72 Hz. Any temporal upscaler amplifies this identically, and XeSS — with its longer, more continuous history — arguably more.

**VR tolerates temporal artifacts badly**, and head turns are the worst case: large disoccluded regions with no history, resolved spatially, softest exactly when the player is moving. JC3's velocity buffer clamps at ±0.125 UV per frame (`fsr.md`), so it *saturates* precisely then. Flat-monitor quality rankings do not transfer to this. One relevant data point cuts against XeSS: the author of the Skyrim VR upscaler — the same code that demonstrates the per-eye base-offset technique — reportedly advises that only DLSS/DLAA and FSR2 work well in VR and that XeSS should not be used. The reason is undocumented and the code nonetheless implements XeSS per-eye, so read it as a quality judgment rather than an API limitation. It is hearsay, but from the one person who has run all three this way.

**Shipping cost.** A 77 MB proprietary binary redistributed alongside the mod (permitted, with notice reproduction) versus MIT source compiled into `fsr-sys` today, plus a second graphics device, cross-API resource sharing, and a second submission timeline to synchronise per frame, per eye.

## What to do instead

**1. Finish the FSR2 upscaler (issue #13).** The context, jitter, motion vectors, and dispatch are built and shipping at 1:1; the FSR side needs no rework. Three concrete pieces remain, none of them upscaler-specific:

- *Decouple render size from swapchain size.* Both currently come from one `scaled_eye_size` (`vr/mod.rs`), so lowering `vr.resolution_scale` lowers the OpenXR swapchain too and the compositor bilinear-stretches it. Upscaling needs the swapchain pinned at the recommended size while the scene renders below it. The mod-owned back buffer (`swapchain-ownership.md`) already broke the render-size/DXGI link; this is the other half of the same idea. Rule out the resample `performance.md` flags first — "noticeably soft at 1.0x" may be that, not resolution.
- *Move where the resolve lands.* `dispatch_eye` derives both render and display size from the post chain's slot texture and copies the result back into that same render-resolution slot. An upscaler produces a display-resolution image, which cannot go back in the ring; it has to resolve into the per-eye target the swapchain blit reads, or the chain's tail has to run at display resolution.
- *Fix bark's previous-frame matrix* before render scale drops, and revisit the deferred hybrid camera-motion-vector path (`fsr.md`, "Precision ceiling"), which attacks the ±0.125 clamp exactly where VR hurts.

**2. Validate foveation (issue #29).** Implemented, on by default, entirely untested in a headset (`foveation.md`'s open items). `performance.md` argues it is the structurally correct fill-rate answer because its saving grows with resolution and it discards pixels the lenses discard anyway; unlike an upscaler it adds no temporal history and no head-motion artifact class, and it has no third-party dependency. Validating something already written beats integrating something new.

**3. Keep the bottleneck in view.** `DrawGBuffer` is 3.56 ms at 1.0x and 3.64 ms at a quarter of the pixels — geometry does not track pixel count, and the GPU sits near 50%. An upscaler buys back post-chain time (1.30 ms at 1.0x) and nothing else, and a *more expensive* upscaler behind an interop layer can plausibly cost more than it returns. This is the strongest argument against spending effort here at all right now, and it applies to finishing FSR2 as much as to adopting XeSS — the difference is that finishing FSR2 is cheap because it is nearly done.

## Verified versus assumed

**Verified (primary sources).** The XeSS D3D11 backend is Intel-hardware-only, per `inc/xess/xess_d3d11.h` and Intel's XeSS-SR developer guide; it ships as a separate 156 KB `libxess_dx11.dll` importing neither d3d11 nor d3d12 and dispatching into `igdext64.dll` via `INTC_D3D11_Xess*`; the cross-vendor `libxess.dll` is 77 MB, delay-imports d3d12, and requires SM 6.4; the SDK is binary-only under the Intel Simplified Software License, redistributable in binary form but not modifiable or reverse-engineerable; the execute-params base coordinates exist in both the D3D11 and D3D12 headers and have since v1.0.0; the input set and the `HIGH_RES_MV` / `USE_NDC_VELOCITY` / `JITTERED_MV` / `INVERTED_DEPTH` flags; XeSS keeps one temporal history per context and is single-thread-affine; Vulkan and D3D11 both arrived in SDK 2.0.1 (2025-03-17), D3D12 having been the only backend from 1.0 through 1.3.1. DXVK's `dxgi_options.cpp` XeSS vendor-hiding workaround and its 2.0.2.68 version gate. OptiScaler's documented D3D11on12 route and its "up to 10-ish %" figure (primary for OptiScaler's own claim; an estimate as a performance number). Intel's Unreal plugin: XeSS-SR supports VR, XeSS-FG does not. PureDark's Skyrim Upscaler VR branch implementing per-eye base offsets on a double-wide SBS D3D11 target. Mod-side: FSR2's dispatch has no region offsets; `split_fullscreen_pass` masks with a scissor and cannot reach compute; render size and swapchain size share one computation; bark's previous-frame velocity matrix is un-reprojected under the collapse.

**Secondary (independent testing, cited above, not re-verified).** XeSS's artifact profile versus DLSS and FSR2, chiefly TechPowerUp's per-game comparison series, whose findings are inconsistent between titles and should be read as such; the TechSpot/Hardware Unboxed "one quality tier" cost finding, which is XeSS **1.0** on Ampere and predates 1.3's rebalancing; TechPowerUp's 10–13% aggregate; that XMX and DP4a are different trained networks rather than one network at two speeds; GE-Proton and Proton-CachyOS XeSS/OptiScaler integration.

The quality evidence is the weakest part of this document and the part most worth redoing if the decision is reopened. It is all flatscreen, mostly per-title, and none of it isolates the behaviours VR punishes (disocclusion under head rotation, per-eye consistency, convergence after a history reset). The reasoning above deliberately does not lean on it.

**Hearsay.** PureDark's reported advice against XeSS in VR — snippet-level only, reason undocumented.

**Not verified, and it is the number that would decide a performance argument.** XeSS DP4a cost in milliseconds on high-end NVIDIA hardware, at any resolution. Nothing credible exists. If this evaluation is reopened, measuring that here is step one.

**Marketing, not evidence.** Intel's "boosts frame rates on all GPUs with SM 6.4 (DP4a) support".

**Corrected mid-evaluation.** An earlier revision asserted that D3D11↔D3D12 interop for XeSS was impractical under Proton and that no report of anyone doing it existed. Both parts were wrong. The recommendation did not change, but its basis moved from "not viable" to "viable and not worth it" — a materially weaker claim, and the one the evidence supports.
